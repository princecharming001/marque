# Style treatments: storytime, comedy, hot take

Load this file once the brief names the style as storytime (a true story told to camera), comedy (a joke, bit or sketch) or hot take (an opinion argued to camera), or a blend. The common blends are comedic storytime and the rant, which is a hot take argued through a story. The file shifts the defaults in `cutting-and-pacing.md`, `framing-and-zooms.md`, `broll.md`, `captions-and-text.md`, `music.md`, `sfx.md` and `endings-loops-ctas.md` without repeating their mechanics. Keep it loaded from the story cut through finishing. In all three styles the viewer came for a person, so doing nothing to the delivery is often the best edit.

## Principles

1. **Name the style, or the blend, before cutting.** *Why:* a 1 s silence is a flaw in a hot take, the effect in a story and the performer's property in a joke.
2. **The delivery is the product; the edit only clears its way.** *Why:* over-editing is our documented failure: a pro made 1 splice where the old engine made 19 [I]. When a strong teller's take passes the radio test, head and tail trims plus 0–2 seams is a complete edit (`cutting-and-pacing.md`, step 9).
3. **Storytime: protect the spell.** *Why:* absorption in a story (transportation) predicts emotional response (ρ = .57) and rises with the viewer's attention (ρ = .29) [L]. In TV studies, even a thematically matched ad broke absorption and was judged worse when it interrupted the story mid-flow (Wang & Calder 2009) [L]. So every insert, zoom or sound must belong to the story and land at a break in it.
4. **Storytime: hook on the stakes, never the twist; keep the performed beat before a reveal.** *Why:* the open question holds a story. In a lab sentence task, words after a silent pause were remembered better, though the pause blunted the brain's surprise response [L, sentences, not stories]. The creator's own beat trades a little surprise for suspense; frozen frames they never performed only add delay, so prefer a take that has the beat.
5. **Comedy: the timing belongs to the performer.** *Why:* punchlines showed no special pause or rate in 20 recorded jokes, and preliminary data suggest the same for professionals [L, small, untrained tellers]. In the sentence study above, a pause before an unexpected word removed the brain's early mismatch (surprise) response [L]. Add no beat before a punchline, trim none the performer chose, and cut only dead air between bits.
6. **Comedy: nothing reveals, covers or stamps the punchline.** *Why:* BBC subtitle rules separate punchlines and clear the screen for reactions [P]; anything on or before the word gives it away.
7. **Comedy: the laugh and the reaction are content.** *Why:* over 99% of 1,200 natural laughs fell at phrase ends [L], and real laughter, spontaneous more than posed, made jokes funnier [L].
8. **Comedy: choose the punchline take on exact wording first, then on aliveness.** *Why:* in Attardo & Pickering's recordings listeners tolerated tellers' slips, yet one substituted punchline word ("mulch" for "sod") "completely destroys the joke" [L, one anecdote].
9. **Hot take: the take is the first line, at the creator's own strength.** *Why:* TikTok's ad guidance puts the proposition in the first 3 s [A, ads]; clickbait headlines lowered perceived credibility in experiments (Kaushal & Vemuri 2021) [L]; and qualifiers are part of the claim.
10. **Hot take: the heat comes from the argument, not the edit.** *Why:* conversation peaks at moderate controversy and falls when discomfort outgrows interest [L]; rage graphics add risk, not insight.
11. **"None" is the default for music, SFX and inserts in all three.** *Why:* voice and face carry these styles, and a bed masks speech and timing (`music.md`).

**Evidence behind the priors:**
- Transportation (132 effect sizes): it rises with identifiable characters (ρ = .20), an imaginable plot (ρ = .29) and lifelikeness (ρ = .27) [L] ([van Laer 2014](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/6755/1/SSRN-id2033192.pdf)).
- Emotional swings: more volatile movies (4,000+) were rated higher, readers kept reading more volatile articles (30,000+), and experiments showed volatility causally raises evaluations [L] ([Berger, Kim & Meyer 2021](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucab010)). This is a reason to keep a story's comic relief and its low point rather than flatten both.
- Hook types: story-teaser hooks averaged 3,602 views (790 clips) and absurd statements 4,452 (782), against 1,973 for shock/surprise hooks, the most used (7,722). Hooks were AI-classified across 34,635 TikTok clips, with 7-day views; correlational [V] ([OpusClip](https://www.opus.pro/research/best-video-hooks-tiktok)).
- Joke timing: mean pause 0.51 s in setups against 0.42 s before punchlines, not significant; prepared and improvised jokes did not differ [L] ([Attardo & Pickering 2011](https://faculty.tamuc.edu/lpickering/Pdfs/Publish_11.pdf)).
- Laughter: over 99% of 1,200 natural laughs fell at phrase or sentence ends [L] ([Provine 1993](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1439-0310.1993.tb00478.x)); added laughter raised funniness ratings of deliberately bad jokes, spontaneous laughter more than posed, in neurotypical and autistic raters alike [L] ([Cai 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2019.05.073)).
- Controversy: discussion rises then falls as controversy grows (an inverted U), because interest and discomfort both rise with it [L] ([Chen & Berger 2013](https://doi.org/10.1086/671465)).
- Sharing: amusement raised video-ad sharing, drama elements (surprise, plot, characters) aroused the emotions that did, and information-focused content lowered it [L] ([Tellis 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022242919841034)). In New York Times articles, high-arousal emotions (awe, anger, anxiety) spread further than sadness [L] ([Berger & Milkman 2012](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.10.0353)).
- Hot-take incentives, not quality targets: each negative headline word raised click-through 2.3% (370M impressions) [L] ([Robertson 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-023-01538-4)). Each out-group term raised share odds 67% and predicted "angry" reactions (2.7M posts, observational) [L] ([Rathje 2021](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2024292118)).

**Reference creators** (examples, not templates):
- **Storytime.** Reesa Teesa's "Who TF Did I Marry?!" ran 50 parts of about 10 min, direct to camera. A critic called it "totally unstaged", and it passed 500M views ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Who_TF_Did_I_Marry%3F)). The edit's job with a strong teller is to stay out of the way.
- **Comedy.** Khaby Lame, TikTok's most-followed creator, is silent: he stitches overcomplicated hacks, and his reaction is the joke ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khaby_Lame)). Brittany Broski's "Kombucha girl" clip is only changing facial expressions ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brittany_Broski)). For Jonathan Bynoe's two-character sketch, TikTok credits "perfectly timed cuts between the two characters", and notes that captions alone would not carry the humour in his voice [A] ([TikTok](https://s3.amazonaws.com/media.mediapost.com/uploads/5_Keys_to_Successful_TikTok_Creator_Ads.pdf)).
- **Hot take.** Three Hormozi advice clips (a listicle, a live Q&A, a call-in) start speech at 0.0 s, hard-cut every 3.5–5.6 s, keep fillers, stammers and audience laughter, and use no b-roll [V] n=3 ([PandaStudio](https://www.writepanda.ai/blog/hormozi-style-shorts-editing)). V Spehar's "Under the Desk News" grew from a video filmed lying under a desk ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V_Spehar)): a signature frame, not b-roll.

## How to decide

1. **Classify from transcript and prosody.** Storytime: past-tense narration, named characters, time markers, a turn. Comedy: setup-punch pairs, the creator's laughs, character switches, callbacks, lists of three. Hot take: an early first-person verdict ("unpopular opinion", "stop doing X"), reasons, a restated verdict. For blends, use storytime structure with comedy timing inside punchline spans; a rant takes the hot-take hook and the storytime body. Record style, measured energy (arousal, wpm) and dials in the brief.
2. **Pin protected word IDs.** Storytime: stakes line, reveal, emotional lines, resolution. Comedy: last setup clause, punchline, button, every laugh at full length, catchphrases. Hot take: the take with its qualifiers, the strongest reason, any spoken question or CTA.
3. **Story cut.**
   - **Storytime.** Cut preamble and keep chronological order. Cut a tangent unless it sets up a character or a later callback. Hook on a stakes drop-in, or a tease naming the stakes but not the outcome. Never pull the twist forward. Between takes, prefer the one where the teller relives the moment over the one where they recite it; emotion outranks polish (Murch's rule of six, `cutting-and-pacing.md`).
   - **Comedy.** Compress the setup to what the punchline needs, but keep a list of three whole. Never move a punchline ahead of its setup. In a two-character sketch, choose a take per line and cut on each switch.
   - **Hot take.** Take first, then the one to three strongest reasons, ending on the restated take or the creator's question. Cut a trailing hedge ("idk, just my opinion") only if the claim's strength is unchanged; if in doubt, keep it and flag it.
4. **Fine cut.** Set gaps from the table. Storytime hides seams inside a thought (a real cutaway or a clearly different framing); comedy and hot takes may embrace jump cuts between beats. Cut a laugh before it starts or after it ends, never through its peak (for long audience laughs, see stand-up clips below).
5. **Finishing, in stage order: framing, inserts, captions, music, SFX.** Inserts by style:
   - Storytime: the creator's own receipts (texts, photos) on a face-kept card, with third parties' names, numbers and faces blurred. Bring them in at a sentence break, never across the climax line. Never stock strangers standing in for real people.
   - Comedy: only a gag the voice sets up, hard-cut on the noun.
   - Hot take: the thing being answered, whole and uncropped, as creator-over-screenshot. For another creator's video, recommend the platform's Stitch or green screen at post time rather than rendering their clip in (originality and licence).
6. **End** per the table and `endings-loops-ctas.md`.
7. **Build one alternate where the risk sits:** storytime stakes hook vs tease hook; comedy with vs without a CTA after the button; hot take spoken-first vs text-led open. Rank with the critic questions.

## When to break it

- **Deadpan.** No punch-ins, SFX or stingers; stillness is the joke.
- **Silent or visual comedy.** Captions may be empty; the reaction hold can run 1–2 s with nothing over the face.
- **Sketches with character switches.** One cut per line is correct, and seams-per-minute does not apply.
- **The cut is the joke.** In Edgar Wright's comedies the cut, the frame and the sound carry gags themselves ([Every Frame a Painting](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3FOzD4Sfgag)) [P]. A smash cut or cutaway gag lands exactly on the beat (`transitions-and-graphics.md`).
- **Serialized stories.** Longer parts and a part-two cliffhanger are fine if part two exists and part one pays off its own hook.
- **Sensitive stories** (grief, abuse, health). No music, SFX, push-ins or sensational hook title. Keep the creator's own coping jokes, and add no comic device.
- **Stand-up clips.** Keep the audience laughter at its natural level. Stage timing waits until a laugh "start[s] to die down" before the next line ([Comic timing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comic_timing)) [P], so to shorten a long laugh, keep its onset and peak and let the next line start as it decays (a J-cut on audio); never cut the peak. In crowd work, keep and caption the audience member.
- **A calm hot take.** Use calm dials: 350–500 ms pauses and no punch-ins.
- **Split-screen gameplay under a story** ("sludge content"). Only on explicit request. It is criticised as overstimulating ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sludge_content)), and ripped gameplay or TV clips carry licence and originality risk.
- **Creator memory** ("memes welcome", "never music") overrides these priors. Licences still apply.

## Worked example

A comedic storytime: medium-high energy, a 4K HLG 60 fps selfie take of 88 s, with two deliveries of the reveal.

| Words | Line | Tag |
|---|---|---|
| w0–14 | "Okay so, um, storytime, this is kind of long but stick with me." | PREAMBLE |
| w15–31 | "Last month my landlord texts me at 2 a.m.: 'Do NOT open your oven.'" | STAKES / HOOK |
| w32–40 | "I don't even use my oven. I store sweaters in it." (creator laughs, 0.9 s) | JOKE + LAUGH |
| w41–48 | "For context, this man communicates exclusively in thumbs-ups." | CHARACTER |
| w49–70 | His boat, and a fishing trip | TANGENT |
| w71–99 | "So I text 'why?' Thumbs up. Seven a.m., there's a knock." (pitch falls, 1.1 s gap) | RISING + BEAT |
| w100–111 | "It's a fire inspector. Holding my sweaters. In a bag labeled 'evidence.'" (laugh, 1.4 s) | REVEAL A |
| w112–124 | "It's a fire inspector holding my sweaters, uh, in a bag that says evidence." | REVEAL B |
| w125–138 | "Anyway. I have a new oven. It's full of shoes." | BUTTON |
| w139–147 | "Follow for part two, it gets worse. Bye!" | CTA + OUTRO |

**Decisions.**
- **Cut w0–14**, so "Last month…" starts at 0.2 s. **Hook = w15–31:** specific, stakes-first, quoting the text. Pulling the fire-inspector line forward was rejected because it spoils the reveal. The hook title, "The 2 a.m. landlord text", is a label, not the joke.
- **Joke 1:** "sweaters" gets its own caption page, and the 0.9 s laugh stays whole.
- **Keep w41–48**, because it sets up the thumbs-up callback. **Cut the boat tangent**, since nothing pays it off.
- **Rising action:** pauses stay near the speaker's ~400 ms median. A +6% push runs over about 6 s from "Seven a.m." through the kept 1.1 s beat, with captions cleared during the beat.
- **Reveal:** take A overrides the later take because "labeled 'evidence'" is sharper and B has a filler before the punch word. "evidence" gets its own page on the word, with no SFX, insert or music. The 1.4 s laugh stays, with the screen clear.
- **Inserts:** the creator's screenshot of the text sits on a face-kept card from 3.0 s, with the landlord's name and number blurred. We ask for a pickup photo of the sweater oven for a visual-gag cutaway that hard-cuts in on "sweaters" and returns to the face as the laugh starts (about 0.7 s), so the reaction stays on screen. Without the photo there is no insert, because stock sweaters are someone else's oven.
- **Ending:** keep the button, with "shoes" as a punch page. Keep the protected "Follow for part two, it gets worse" and cut "Bye!". The CTA steps on the button, so a no-CTA alternate goes to the creator, and the brief confirms part two exists. No music, no SFX.

Result: about 54 s, 2 internal seams, 1 push, 1 card, 0–1 cutaways.

**The same engine on a hot take.** The line "Splitting the bill on a first date is fine, actually" opens at 0.1 s. The hook title quotes it with "actually" intact, followed by a 0.5 s beat. Two reasons stay and a weaker third goes. One punch-in lands on "fine", there is no music, and the video ends 0.4 s after "Fight me in the comments."

## Anti-patterns

- A suspense bed, "dun-dun" stingers or a riser under a storytime reveal.
- Hook text, a title or an insert that gives away the twist or the punchline.
- Stock strangers standing in for real people in the story, or unblurred names and numbers in screenshots.
- Trimming the dramatic pause before a reveal, faking one with frozen frames, or adding one before a punchline.
- Cutting mid-laugh, or covering the reaction with changing text.
- A vine boom, record scratch or zoom on every joke; SFX on the punch word; explaining the joke in text.
- Speeding up a punchline, or reordering it into the hook.
- A canned laugh track presented as a real audience.
- Hardening "for most people" into "everyone", or softening a stated take into a question.
- Rage graphics: red arrows, "EXPOSED" stamps, a cropped quote that misrepresents what is being answered.
- Burying the take under context, or putting a music bed under an opinion so it plays like an ad.

## Critic questions

Answer each yes or no from the rendered video.

1. Does the stakes line, premise or take arrive within about 2.5 s, without giving away the twist or the punchline?
2. Is every punchline and reveal kept off screen (captions, titles, inserts) until it is spoken?
3. Does every punchline play at 1.0× with no SFX or music hit on the word itself, and no insert on it unless the insert is the gag?
4. Is every laugh whole, cut cleanly before it starts, or (a long audience laugh) overlapped by the next line only after its peak?
5. After each punchline, is the face visible with no changing text over the reaction?
6. Does the creator's own beat before each storytime reveal survive, with no manufactured beat anywhere?
7. Is every insert a real, specific thing from the story or the actual thing being answered, with third parties' details blurred?
8. Does the on-screen hot-take text match the spoken claim, with no qualifier removed and no certainty added?
9. Muted, can a viewer follow the joke or the take from face plus captions? (Humour that lives in the voice's timbre will not fully survive; the words must still be captioned verbatim and on time.)
10. Does the video end within about 1 s of the button, resolution, restated take or spoken CTA?
11. Does edit intensity (seams, zooms, SFX) match the creator's energy, so that deadpan stays still?
12. Is the video free of music under dialogue jokes and of suspense cues under the story, unless the brief names a job for them?

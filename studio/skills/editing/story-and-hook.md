# Story structure and the hook

Load this file at the brief and story-cut stages, and whenever a critic flags the opening, the structure or a missing payoff. It covers finding the one idea in a talking-head take, the brief, beats, structural cuts (preamble, tangents, repetition), reordering, and the first 1–3 seconds: hook type, take choice, cold opens and alternates. Pauses and seams live in `cutting-and-pacing.md`, endings in `endings-loops-ctas.md`, hook-title styling in `captions-and-text.md`. The story cut is words and order only; finishing never rescues a weak structure.

## Principles

1. **One video, one idea.** *Why:* in lab multimedia lessons, people learned more deeply when extraneous material was removed (coherence principle: 18 of 19 tests, median d = 0.86; Mayer 2021, via Mayer 2023) [L], and a second idea splits the promise the hook made.
2. **Find the payoff before you choose the hook.** *Why:* the hook is a promise, so you can only pick it once you know what the video delivers. Jenny Hoyos writes the hook and the last line first [P].
3. **The first kept line is content, never preamble.** *Why:* most skips happen within the first 5 s [A]. A greeting spends that window on nothing.
4. **Cut throat-clearing, keep direct address.** *Why:* in TikTok's ad research, a person on screen in the first 2 s raised "hooking power" by 50%, and ads with speech addressed to the viewer were 1.5× as likely to hook [A]. "Hey guys, so today" addresses the viewer but carries no information.
5. **Open a gap the body closes.** *Why:* curiosity comes from a salient gap between what the viewer knows and wants to know [L], and it peaks when they half-know the answer; answers that surprise a curious viewer are remembered better [L]. Do not lean on the "Zeigarnik open loop": a 2025 meta-analysis found no memory advantage for unfinished tasks, only a general urge to resume interrupted ones, and on lab tasks, not viewing [L].
6. **Never promise what the cut does not deliver.** *Why:* in two online experiments, clickbait headlines tended to lower perceived credibility and quality [L]. MrBeast's production memo treats the title and thumbnail as expectations the video must meet [P]. An oversold hook trains viewers to swipe this creator.
7. **Link beats with "but" or "therefore", never "and then".** *Why:* causation is what turns a sequence into a story (Parker and Stone) [P]. An "and then" beat is a cut candidate.
8. **Keep the creator's causal order. Reorder only the open.** *Why:* the speaker built the body's reference chain ("that", "so", "which is why"), and moving body beats breaks it. Pulling one standalone line to the top is the bounded, classic exception [V].
9. **The story cut must pass the radio test.** *Why:* documentary and interview editors often build an audio-only "radio cut" first, and the DOAC trailer editor writes the story as a transcript document first, because a weak story cannot hide there [P]. A talking head is almost all speech.
10. **The opening must also pass a mute test.** *Why:* 69% of US adults report watching with sound off in public [V], while 88% of TikTok users call sound vital (Nielsen for TikTok, 2020) [A]. The open has to work both ways.
11. **Emotion outranks tidiness.** *Why:* when choosing a cut, Murch ranks emotion (51%) above story (23%) and rhythm (10%) [P]. A raw or funny aside that makes the creator human can beat a cleaner cut.
12. **Changing nothing is a valid decision.** *Why:* over-editing is our documented failure; on a calibration take a pro made 1 splice where the old engine made 19 [I]. If the creator already opens on the point and flows, leave it.

## How to decide

1. **Read everything before cutting.** Walk the transcript with the take index. Mark re-deliveries, false starts (a line is a false start only if a later re-delivery exists), the CTA, laughs and catchphrases.
2. **Write the brief.**
   - **One idea**, in one sentence of 25 words or fewer. If you cannot write it, the take holds two ideas: keep the stronger and note the other for the creator.
   - **Audience** and what they already believe.
   - **Goal:** what the viewer should think, feel or do.
   - **Payoff:** the word-ID range that delivers the idea.
   - **Promise:** what the hook commits to; the payoff must answer it.
   - **Protected IDs:** CTA, catchphrase, laughs.
   - **Style profile, measured energy, and length** (the sum of kept beats, never a quota).
3. **Map beats.** Tag each sentence: PREAMBLE, HOOK-CANDIDATE, CONTEXT, STAKES, POINT, PROOF, TURN, PAYOFF, CTA, TANGENT, REPEAT, FALSE-START, OUTRO. Read the kept beats in order and say "but" or "therefore" between them. Where only "and then" fits, cut or merge. Then check the gap stays open: if the body answers the hook in the first third, prefer ending sooner, or use the creator's own TURN ("but here's the catch") as a second, smaller gap. The DOAC trailer editor stacks hooks to hold longer clips [P]; never invent one.
4. **Make structural cuts on word-ID ranges.**
   - **Preamble:** greetings, "so today I want to talk about", "in this video", self-introduction. Keep a credential only if the claim needs it, as one clause after the hook.
   - **Superseded takes:** keep the chosen delivery, cut the rest.
   - **Tangents:** cut if the payoff lands without them, unless they carry emotion, humor or identity the audience comes for (check creator memory).
   - **Repetition:** keep the best statement of each point, plus deliberate emphasis or a closing callback.
   - **After the payoff:** cut everything but the spoken CTA; validators forbid deleting that.
5. **Choose the hook.** Candidates: the first real content line, each re-delivery of it, the strongest standalone line anywhere (claim, number, result, stakes), and the payoff when showing the result first keeps a gap. Score each yes/no:
   - **Clear:** a stranger knows the topic after one listen.
   - **Specific:** a concrete noun, number or consequence, not "this one thing".
   - **Relevant:** it names or implies the audience ("you", "in your twenties").
   - **Gap:** it raises a question the body answers.
   - **Honest:** the payoff delivers it.
   - **Standalone:** no leading "so/and/because" and no "this/that/it" pointing backwards.
   - **Delivery:** fluent first five words, no filler, falling pitch at the end, energy at or above the body median, eyes to lens.
   - **Short:** one sentence; the gap is set by about 6 s.

   Hook types for talking heads:
   - **Bold claim / verdict:** "Six months of savings is the wrong first goal."
   - **Question:** self-referencing and specific. "Why does your budget die by the 15th?" beats "Did you know…?"
   - **Result-first:** the outcome, then how. Default for tutorials and transformations. Spoilers did not lower enjoyment in lab studies, but a replication found some cut suspense [L]: reveal results, not story twists.
   - **Pattern break:** contradicts a common belief, or an unexpected line or real action; never a synthetic glitch.
   - **Visual hook:** the object, result or demo in frame at 0 s. The face itself is the default visual hook.
   - **Text hook:** a hook title restating or sharpening the spoken promise; it supports a spoken hook, never replaces one.
   - **Stakes drop-in (storytime):** start mid-scene at the moment something could go wrong.
6. **Pick among hook takes.** Prior: the last complete delivery; override on the delivery check. Avoid splicing two takes inside the hook sentence: pitch, energy and framing rarely match, and the seam lands where scrutiny is highest. If none passes, use the best one whole. The rare exception is a join at a phrase boundary, pitch-matched and covered by a scale change, that a blind listen cannot find.
7. **Reorder only when it wins.** A pull-forward cold open moves one standalone range to the head. Remove its original occurrence unless the echo is deliberate. Confirm clean gaps (≥150 ms) at both ends. Treat the jump back as a beat change (hard cut by default; see `cutting-and-pacing.md`). If the line needs its setup to make sense, it is not a cold open.
8. **Set frame 0 and the lead-in.** "Treat your intro like a thumbnail" (Galloway) [V]: start on a still face with eyes open, never a black frame, logo or slate. Land the first word at 0.1–0.5 s (validators add onset padding).
9. **Run the radio test** on the audio-only render: does it make sense with no picture, is the promise paid off, does any line refer to something unseen? Then the **mute test** on the first 3 s: can face plus hook title carry the topic?
10. **Build alternates and hand off.** Produce 2–3 openings of different types; the critic ranks them by position-swapped pairwise comparison. If only one real hook exists, ship one; never manufacture alternates. Ship the winner, keep the rest as deliverables, and for high stakes let the creator pick or test via Instagram Trial Reels. Flag for finishing: hook at 1.0×; hook title only if it passes the mute test; no full-screen cutaway for ~1.5–3 s unless it is a visual hook (`broll.md`); no SFX on the first word (`sfx.md`); voice-only hook or a bed from frame one (`music.md`); a punch-in on the jump back from a cold open is optional, not required.

## When to break it

- **Storytime:** open on the stakes, not a claim. Never pull the twist forward.
- **Comedy:** the setup is the hook. Never pull the punchline forward, and never "sharpen" a joke into a claim.
- **Tutorials and transformations:** show the finished result first; the picture carries the hook. The radio test (whose known blind spot is non-verbal moments [P]) relaxes for demo stretches, but words must not point at things the viewer cannot see.
- **A signature greeting** that is the creator's brand can stay if it lasts about 1 s or less.
- **Sophisticated audiences** may need a 4–6 s setup; clarity beats a stopwatch.
- **Podcast clips:** a pull-forward is standard when the line stands alone; DOAC even mutes its key words to hold the gap (n=3) [V].
- **Vulnerable or sincere content:** start on the true moment; never manufacture a hook.
- **Flagged afterthoughts:** if the creator says "oh, I should have said…" and the line stands alone, move it to where it belongs. That restores their intended order; it is not reshuffling.
- **The creator's opening is already strong:** change nothing.

## Worked example

A finance creator, medium-high energy, 94 s single take, 9:16.

| Words (raw s) | Line | Beat |
|---|---|---|
| w0–15 (0–4.6) | "Okay, um, hey guys. So today I wanted to talk about something you keep asking about," | PREAMBLE |
| w16–31 (4.6–9.8) | "which is emergency funds. So, like, everyone says you need six months of expenses. Six months." | CONTEXT |
| w32–45 (9.8–16) | "And honestly that advice is kind of broken for most people in their twenties…" | FALSE-START |
| w46–51 (16–19) | "Wait, let me say that again." | FALSE-START |
| w52–66 (19–24) | "For most people in their twenties, six months of savings is the wrong first goal." | HOOK-CANDIDATE A |
| w67–110 (24–40) | "Because if you have credit card debt at 24%, every dollar sitting in savings at 4% is costing you 20 cents a year…" | PROOF |
| w111–142 (40–52) | Roommate who names her savings accounts after her cats | TANGENT |
| w143–181 (52–68) | "Do one month first. One month of bare-bones expenses. Then throw everything at the debt. Then build to three, then six." | POINT |
| w182–199 (68–78) | "So yeah, basically, like I said, six months isn't the first goal." | REPEAT |
| w200–214 (78–86) | "One month first. Debt second. Six months last. That order is the whole trick." | PAYOFF |
| w215–227 (86–94) | "Follow for part two where I show you my actual spreadsheet. Okay, bye!" | CTA + OUTRO |

**Brief.** One idea: "In your twenties, save one month first, kill high-interest debt, then build to six." Audience: twenty-somethings with card debt who know the six-month rule. Goal: change the order they save in. Payoff: w200–214. Protected: w215–225 (CTA).

**Hook candidates.**
- **A (w52):** passes all eight checks. It names the audience, contradicts a known rule and opens "then what is?".
- **B:** pull-forward of the math line, minus "Because" (w67; clean 180 ms gap). Specific and self-referencing, but it is the proof, so it must leave the body or play twice.
- **C:** the natural open, "Everyone says you need six months of expenses." The topic is clear by 2.5 s, but the verdict lands only at about 8 s and the open needs a seam.

**Cut.** Cut w0–51: preamble, context and both false starts go with no reorder, since A flows straight into the proof. Cut w111–142, the tangent (mildly funny but off the idea; noted for creator memory). Cut w182–199; the payoff says it better. Cut w226–227 ("Okay, bye!").

Result: about 50 s, 2 internal seams, payoff at about 37 s (74%), first word 0.2 s after a still, eyes-up frame 0.

**Not done, on purpose.** No reorder, no speed change, no splice inside A, no trims inside the proof, and body fillers left to the fine cut, which may keep most of them.

**Tests.** Radio test passes: every beat links by "because" or "therefore". Mute test passes with the hook title "6 months saved isn't step one" (6 words, 0–3 s).

**Alternates.** Ship A as main and B as the alternate: a number-first hook is the higher-variance bet for a finance audience. C is rejected: a slower verdict and an extra seam.

## Anti-patterns

- Opening on a greeting, logo, "in this video" or b-roll instead of the face; in DOAC's Facebook tests, opening on the guest in the chair always won [P].
- A cold open that depends on context ("And that's why it failed"), or one that plays twice because the original was left in.
- A hook the video never pays off, or a hook far stronger than the video.
- Chasing a stopwatch: clipping the hook sentence until it is vague, or speeding it up.
- Stacking whoosh, zoom, emoji, flashing text and a question on frame 0. In TV ads, scattered viewer attention and centrally placed logos both predicted zapping (eye tracking, ~2,000 viewers) [L]; irrelevant attention-grabbers cost comprehension [L].
- Fake pattern interrupts (glitches, notification sounds, random memes), or hooks spliced from several takes mid-sentence.
- Shuffling body beats "for retention" until the causality reads "and then".
- Scrubbing every aside until the creator sounds scripted, or splicing every sentence (19 where 1 would do).
- Trailing 15 s after the payoff, or deleting the CTA the creator actually said.
- Choosing a take because it came first, or loudest despite a stumble. Generic "Did you know…?" questions.

## Critic questions

Answer each yes or no from the rendered video.

1. Does the first spoken word (or an on-screen payoff) arrive within 2.5 s, with no greeting, logo or preamble first?
2. After the first 3 s, could a first-time viewer name the topic?
3. Does the opening line make sense on its own, without any earlier context?
4. With sound off, do the first 3 s (face, hook text, visual) communicate the topic?
5. With picture off, does the whole video make sense beat to beat?
6. Is the hook's promise explicitly paid off later in the video?
7. Can you state the video's single idea in one sentence?
8. Is every sentence heard only once, apart from deliberate emphasis or callbacks?
9. Could any stretch be removed without the payoff losing meaning?
10. Is the hook one fluent, continuous delivery with a confident (falling) finish?
11. Does the video end within about 0.5 s of the last spoken word (payoff or CTA), with no outro, unless a held visual shows something new?
12. Did the cut keep the creator's personality moments that serve the idea?
13. For a video over ~40 s, is a question still open at the midpoint?
14. If the raw opening already passed checks 1–4, did the edit leave it alone?
